Game Theory Reading

The four strategy games in Labs are small versions of problems we see in real deals. These are the books and papers we would hand you after you play them.

Start here

Two readings that set up everything else: what game theory is for, and how to use it without the math.

  • R-01Book

    The Art of Strategy

    Avinash Dixit and Barry Nalebuff · 2008

    If you read one book on this list, make it this one: sequential moves, the Prisoner’s Dilemma and mixed strategies in plain language, and the spine for every game on this page.

  • R-02Essay

    What Is Game Theory Trying to Accomplish?

    Robert Aumann · 1985

    A Nobel laureate’s short case that a theory earns its keep by what it helps you see, which is the right expectation to carry into everything else here.

Solve the Move

Find the move that changes the position, then prove it holds.

  • R-03Book

    Thinking, Fast and Slow

    Daniel Kahneman · 2011

    Every puzzle is a contest between the move that jumps out and the line you actually calculate, and this is the best account we know of why the first one feels so right and so often is not.

Nim

A game that looks like judgment and turns out to be arithmetic.

  • R-04Reference

    Algorithmic Game Theory

    Noam Nisan, Tim Roughgarden, Éva Tardos and Vijay Vazirani, eds. · 2007

    Nim is the classic case of strategy that reduces to a calculation, and this graduate reference is where that idea goes next, from computing equilibria to designing the rules of an auction; dense, so dip into chapters rather than read it cover to cover.

Cooperate or Undercut

Why cooperation can be the rational choice once the game is played more than once.

  • R-05Book

    The Evolution of Cooperation

    Robert Axelrod · 1984

    The computer tournaments behind the strategies in our game, and the clearest case for opening nice, answering an undercut, and forgiving fast.

  • R-06Essays

    Metamagical Themas

    Douglas Hofstadter · 1985

    Read the Prisoner’s Dilemma essays: Hofstadter works the payoff matrix through by hand and takes Axelrod’s results somewhere the tournament book does not, into what it means to reason like your opponent.

The Winner’s Curse

Winning a competitive bid can mean you were the most optimistic person in the room.

  • R-07Book

    The Winner’s Curse

    Richard Thaler · 1992

    The book our game is named for, and the evidence that smart, well-informed bidders overpay in common-value auctions far more often than the theory says they should.

  • R-08Paper

    Epsilon Theory: Viewing Capital Markets through the Lenses of Game Theory and History

    Ben Hunt · 2013

    An auction price reflects what bidders believe other bidders believe, not only what the business is worth, and this paper is the sharpest treatment we know of that gap between the story and the fundamentals.

  • R-09Book

    Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment

    Daniel Kahneman, Olivier Sibony and Cass R. Sunstein · 2021

    Every bidder in our game holds a noisy estimate of the same value, and this book explains why that scatter is larger than anyone expects and what disciplined people do about it.
